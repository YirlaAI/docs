Someone on your paid media team just left, and now you're staring at a live campaign that's spending money on rules nobody wrote down. A real campaign handoff process is what stands between "we'll figure it out" and three weeks of guessing why a bid is set where it is or why a certain audience was carved out of retargeting. If you're reading this because a handoff is already underway, the panic is normal. So is the fact that most of what feels lost right now can be rebuilt with a system, not a miracle.

## What Actually Gets Lost When a Campaign Manager Leaves

The budget lines survive. The reasoning behind them doesn't. When someone leaves without a documented handoff, what disappears first is the logic: why a campaign excludes a list of forty domains, why a bid strategy sits two dollars above the platform's suggestion, why a particular job title got suppressed from a retargeting pool six months ago after a client complained. None of that lives in the ad platform. It lives in a person's head, in a Slack thread that's since scrolled off, or in a meeting nobody took notes in.

SHRM makes the financial case plainly: replacing an employee typically costs between 50 and 200 percent of their annual salary, and that estimate is before you count what it takes to relearn everything that walked out the door with them, as [SHRM notes in its analysis of institutional knowledge loss](https://www.shrm.org/executive-network/insights/myth-replaceability-preparing-loss-key-employees). For a campaign manager, that cost shows up in the first week, when someone else has to decide whether it's safe to touch a live campaign without knowing what happens if they do.

The specific things that go missing tend to follow a pattern:

- Naming logic, why campaigns and ad groups are structured and labeled the way they are, and what the abbreviations mean.
- Exclusion lists, which audiences, domains, or job titles were intentionally cut, and the reason each one was added.
- Bid and budget rationale, the thinking behind a manual bid, a budget cap, or a pacing decision that looks arbitrary from the outside.
- Testing history, what's already been tried and ruled out, so the new owner doesn't burn a quarter re-running a test that already failed.
- Stakeholder context, which internal team or client asked for what, and which requests are still open.

None of this disappears on purpose. It accumulates in small decisions made under deadline, and nobody writes it down because the person making the call assumes they'll still be around to explain it later.

Exclusion lists tend to be the riskiest gap, because removing one looks safe from the outside. A new manager sees a suppressed audience segment with no reason attached, and quietly turns it back on to chase volume. Three weeks later a client is asking why their own employees are seeing the ad again, or why a competitor's domain showed up in the click report. The fix was never complicated. The context just wasn't written anywhere a new owner could find it.

## A Handoff Documentation Template That Actually Gets Used

The templates that fail are the ones built for someone else's ideal workflow: twelve fields, three approval steps, a wiki page nobody opens. A handoff document only works if it takes less time to fill out than the disaster it prevents. Keep it to one page per active campaign, organized around five sections:

- Campaign purpose and owner, the goal it's tied to and who inherits it.
- Naming and structure key, what the campaign name segments mean and how new ad groups should be labeled going forward.
- Exclusions and suppressions, every list currently in use, where it lives, and why it was added.
- Bid, budget, and pacing notes, current settings and the last time they changed, with the reason.
- Open threads, pending requests, unresolved questions, or experiments still in progress.

Pair this template with a marketing team transition plan that names a specific point person before someone leaves, not after they've already given notice. That person's job during the transition is to sit down with the departing manager, ask the obvious questions out loud, and catch every sentence that starts with "oh, I just remember that." Those sentences are where the real risk lives, and they almost never make it into a document unless someone is actively listening for them.

It's worth resisting the urge to make this template exhaustive. A twenty-page campaign bible sounds thorough and gets skimmed once, if that. A one-page document per campaign gets read because it's fast to scan under pressure, which is exactly the condition it needs to perform under. If a campaign genuinely needs more context than one page can hold, that's usually a sign the campaign itself has grown too complex for one person to own anyway, and a good team lead treats that as a separate problem worth solving on its own.

## Making Documentation Part of the Weekly Workflow, Not a Fire Drill

A handoff document only holds up if it stays current, and it will only stay current if updating it isn't a separate task competing for attention during a busy week. Build campaign documentation for handoffs into the cadence you already run:

- During weekly campaign reviews, log any exclusion added, bid changed, or budget shifted, along with the reason, directly in the document.
- During onboarding for a new hire, have them read the current handoff doc before touching a live account, and correct anything that's gone stale.
- During quarterly account audits, treat outdated documentation as a finding, the same way you'd flag a broken conversion pixel.

This is where a lot of teams reach for a shared spreadsheet and watch it rot within a month. The format isn't the problem. Manual logs depend on someone remembering to update them after the fact, which is exactly the habit that breaks under deadline pressure.

Weekly one-on-ones are a natural place to reinforce this without adding a meeting. Spend the last five minutes asking what changed in the account this week and why, and have the person update the handoff doc while it's still fresh instead of promising to get to it later. Mentoring a team member on documentation habits early, while they're still new, is a lot easier than trying to extract two years of undocumented decisions out of them during their final week.

This is where a passive layer helps more than another process to maintain. Yirla keeps a running change history on every connected campaign, who changed what and when, so the reasoning behind a bid or an exclusion doesn't depend entirely on someone's memory or a doc they forgot to touch. It's the same instinct behind [keeping a change log your whole team can actually use](https://www.yirla.com/blog/how-to-build-a-linkedin-ad-change-log-your-whole-team-can-actually-use), except it captures itself as changes happen instead of waiting for someone to write them down. Paired with a one-page handoff document, it gives the new owner two sources of truth instead of one person's memory.

## FAQ

**What should a campaign handoff document include?**
Campaign purpose and owner, naming and structure key, exclusions and suppressions with reasons, bid/budget/pacing notes, and open threads or unresolved questions.

**How do you keep handoff documentation from going stale?**
Build updates into the workflow you already run — log changes during weekly campaign reviews, review the doc during new-hire onboarding, and flag outdated docs during quarterly account audits.

**What's the riskiest gap when a campaign manager leaves without documentation?**
Exclusion lists — a new manager may re-enable a suppressed audience or domain with no context, reintroducing a problem that was already solved.

**Should a handoff document be exhaustive?**
No. A one-page document per campaign that's actually read under pressure is more useful than a twenty-page document that gets skimmed once.

Losing a good team member is hard on its own, before you even get to the campaigns. It's fair to feel that, and it's fair to give the person leaving a genuine send-off instead of spending their last two weeks extracting institutional knowledge out of them. A documented campaign handoff process protects the work without turning someone's exit into an audit, and paid media team turnover is a matter of when, not a matter of if.

Start small. Pick the one campaign that would hurt the most to lose context on, write the one-page doc this week, and build the habit before the next departure forces it.
